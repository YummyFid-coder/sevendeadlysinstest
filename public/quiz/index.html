Which sin gets the last word?

Suppose you lend a friend a battered cookbook. Months later you walk into their kitchen and find it open beside a pan of cornbread. They have cooked from it so often that it falls open to your favorite recipe. Are you pleased? Do you suddenly want the book back? Would it matter if they told everyone at the table who taught them to make that dish? A single pan of cornbread can produce quite a revealing argument inside your head.

This seven deadly sins quiz gives you twenty-one small decisions like that one. The statements turn on details you can usually remember. Think of the time you kept something, put something off, or sent a message you had already rewritten twice. Pick the answer that best fits what you did. If a situation is new to you, picture the person you would be with and the real choices you would have. An ordinary Tuesday often makes a better reference point than your best birthday or your worst morning at work.

Find the detail that tips your answer

At a neighborhood fundraiser, you bring trays of sandwiches and stay late to wash up. On Monday someone posts a thank-you that mentions another volunteer by name. You catch yourself rereading it. What would make you close the message and get on with your day? If a line about your sandwiches would do it, you may be feeling the tug of pride. If you keep wishing you had the other volunteer's easy manner with strangers, even after someone thanks you personally, envy makes more sense of your reaction.

Wrath can begin in that same kitchen. Imagine someone drops a tray, laughs, and leaves the mess on the floor. You quite reasonably want a broom and another pair of hands. An hour later the floor is clean and the helper has come back to make amends. Are you still inventing sharp things to say to them on the drive home? That extra hour is a clue. A useful way to answer a statement about anger is to follow the feeling beyond the moment when the practical problem ends.

What are you trying to keep?

Two people linger at a flea market after their friends are ready to leave. One has found a crate of old records. There is a studio album they already own, yet they want the spare copy too, especially now that another shopper has picked it up. The other person has finished shopping. They simply love wandering the aisles and have talked their friends into one more lap three times already. Both people are holding up the carpool. They could give quite different answers to the questions about greed and gluttony.

For greed, pay attention to ownership. Would you feel just as settled if you knew somebody else was looking after the thing you wanted, or does it have to be in your own hands? For gluttony, consider how you handle a good stopping point. There is nothing unusual about loving an old song. The interesting moment comes when your friends are at the exit, the last track has ended, and you are already asking the seller to play the other side.

Notice what gets moved on your calendar

Sloth and lust can each clear an evening's schedule. Think about a chore that took you all week to start and ten minutes to finish. If you had the supplies, a spare hour, and enough energy on Tuesday, what made Friday seem like such a promising alternative? The questions about sloth leave room for a tired person to rest. Look for the chore that kept moving from one empty square to the next even on a week when you felt fine.

For lust, picture the first few days of a crush. A text arrives while you are halfway through picking a movie with a friend. Maybe you put the phone down and forget about it until the credits. Maybe you spend half the movie planning your reply. Both versions of the evening can end with exactly the same text. How much of your attention went into getting there? Concrete details like the scene you missed tend to be easier to remember than how distracted you felt on a scale of one to ten.

Read your leading result

Each of the seven categories has three statements. A choice earns a point when it follows the impulse a statement describes. Some statements describe letting that impulse pass, so either button can earn a point. The result ranks all seven categories by how many matches you made. A single leader comes with a short description you can compare with the examples you had in mind. You might recognize yourself in the description right away or remember one question you would answer differently on a quieter day.

When two or more categories share the highest score, the result names them together. Give each one a separate example from the past month. If pride and wrath tie, recall one time you wanted credit and one time an insult stayed with you. Which incident did you think about again the next morning? Would you tell a friend about one more readily than the other? Those details give a tie a little texture, and they give you a good place to start if you compare results with someone who knows you well.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose between Agree and Disagree?

Choose Agree when the statement fits your usual reaction and Disagree when it feels off. If you are torn, think of the last time you faced a similar situation.

How does the quiz choose my leading sin?

Each sin has three statements. The category with the most matching answers leads your result. If several categories share the highest score, you will see their names together.

What happens if none of my answers earns a point?

You will see all seven bars at zero and a message explaining that none of the statements matched in the scored direction.

Can I try the questions again?

Yes. Select Take the quiz again below the scores to clear your answers and return to the first statement.
